GPU physics

A physics engine advances a model of a world through time. For rigid bodies, that means predicting motion under forces and finding impulses that prevent objects from passing through each other while allowing bouncing, sliding, rolling and constrained movement. The difficult part is making those interactions stable and fast when many bodies touch.

luma.gl's experimental @luma.gl/gpgpu/gpu-physics module approaches physics as a GPU computation pipeline. It reuses sorting, scanning and command-graph infrastructure, keeps simulation state in buffers, and makes that state available to rendering. The long-term goal is a state-of-the-art GPU rigid-body library. Complete, performant support for a pinned glTF physics proposal profile is the first qualification milestone, not a claim about current coverage.

This page explains the architecture rather than prescribing an application API. See the API reference for API details and limitations, and the roadmap for implementation status and qualification gates.

Bodies, shapes and rendered objects​

A body stores position, orientation, linear and angular velocity, mass, center of mass and inertia. Inertia describes resistance to rotation; for general bodies it is a tensor, so the response depends on the axis and orientation. An impulse away from the center of mass changes both linear and angular motion.

A collider attaches a collision shape to a body, possibly at an offset. Several colliders can form one compound body. A sphere or capsule can be described analytically with a few parameters; it does not need a triangle mesh for collision. Convex hulls and nonconvex triangle meshes represent more general geometry at greater computational cost.

The render mesh is a separate representation. Its materials, textures and tessellation do not determine the collision shape. A visually detailed object can have a simple collider, but that approximation must be intentional: replacing a hollow object with its convex hull closes its holes and changes its behavior.

Body modeWho determines motion?Typical use
StaticApplication places it; dynamics does not move itFloors and walls
KinematicApplication supplies motion; contacts affect dynamic bodiesMoving platforms
DynamicForces, impulses and constraints determine motionFalling and colliding objects

A trigger detects overlap without generating a contact response. Collision filters decide which pairs are eligible for interaction. Consequently, two visible objects passing through each other can be correct when their filters exclude collision; the same symptom can also indicate unsupported geometry, exhausted capacity or missed fast motion.

What happens during a simulation step?​

The exact ordering varies between solvers, but a typical velocity-based rigid-body step contains these stages:

StagePurposeMain challenge
Apply inputs and forcesUpdate velocities from gravity, forces and user commandsConsistent units, mass and time
Broad phaseFind pairs whose bounding volumes could overlapAvoid testing every possible pair
Narrow phaseCompute actual contacts for candidate shape pairsRobust geometry and contact normals
Build constraintsTurn contacts and joints into equations for allowable motionPersistent contact identities and coupled bodies
SolveIteratively compute normal, friction and joint impulsesConvergence without adding energy
Integrate and stabilizeAdvance positions and orientations; control accumulated errorPenetration and joint drift
Publish and maintainExpose poses/events, update caches and sleep stateCorrect ownership and synchronization

An all-pairs broad phase tests roughly bodyCount² / 2 pairs. Spatial grids and bounding volume hierarchies eliminate distant candidates before expensive shape tests. They do not eliminate the cost of a genuinely dense pile: many nearby objects still require work.

The narrow phase may use analytic sphere tests, the separating axis theorem for boxes, or support-mapping algorithms such as GJK and EPA for convex shapes. Triangle meshes need acceleration structures to avoid testing every triangle. Generating one overlap result is not enough for stable stacking: a contact manifold retains a small set of contact points that represent the supporting surface.

The solver applies impulses to prevent inward motion at contacts. Restitution models bounce; friction limits tangential impulses according to the normal response. Rolling and spinning resistance are additional material choices. Ordinary contact friction alone does not guarantee that an ideally rolling sphere will eventually stop.

Iterative solvers make repeated passes because changing one body's velocity affects its other contacts. Warm starting reuses previous contact impulses to accelerate convergence. Persistent identities matter: reusing an impulse for a different geometric feature can produce a false kick.

These concepts also appear in CPU engines; the Box2D simulation guide provides background on bodies, contacts, joints, substeps and sleeping. Its implementation is 2D; it is a conceptual reference rather than the specification for luma.gl's 3D solver.

Time, fast motion and sleeping​

Physics should advance in fixed simulation steps, independently of render frame duration. An application accumulates elapsed time, schedules a bounded number of fixed steps, and may interpolate published poses for rendering. It must choose an explicit overload policy rather than submit an ever-growing backlog of simulation work.

Substeps divide a time interval into smaller intervals. Solver iterations refine the constraints within a step. Increasing one does not have the same effect as increasing the other, and both affect cost.

Discrete collision detection examines sampled configurations. A fast ball can cross a thin wall between samples without overlapping it at either end. Continuous collision detection (CCD) considers swept motion and, depending on the method, finds impact times or constructs predictive contacts. Robust CCD must also handle rotation, repeated impacts and bounded work. Smaller steps reduce risk but do not constitute a general CCD guarantee. The bounded continuous sphere path does not establish general rigid-body CCD coverage.

Sleeping skips work for bodies that have settled. Bodies connected through contacts or joints form islands that often need to sleep and wake together. A shared static floor must not connect every resting object into one dynamic island. Sleeping is an optimization; it must not change the response when a moving support or new impact should wake a body.

The luma.gl approach​

There are currently two related paths, with different maturity and scope:

  • GPUSphereWorld specializes discovery and response for spheres. It uses a hashed grid, sort/scan operations and bounded contact storage, with out-of-place iterative solving. Its state buffers can feed rendering directly.
  • GPURigidBodyWorld handles more general shapes and mass properties. Its ordered GPU solver remains the default reference path. It includes hierarchical discovery, sparse persistent contacts and an opt-in parallel solver; these foundations do not yet establish fully qualified large-world general rigid-body simulation.

Independent state streams, such as positions, orientations and velocities, use separate buffers: a structure-of-arrays layout. Passes can bind the state they need, and renderers can consume poses without reconstructing CPU objects. Contact and solver records can use packed layouts where measurements justify them; one layout is not optimal for every pass.

The GPU command graph expresses dependencies and resource lifetimes. Sorting, scanning, contact generation and solving become reusable operations. A logical solve operation can encode multiple dispatches: fewer inspector nodes need not mean fewer GPU kernels or less work. The timeline must still expose the actual cost.

The general-body path uses a size-aware hierarchical grid to avoid forcing small shapes and very large colliders into one cell scale. Sparse contact caches replace storage proportional to all possible body pairs. Small all-pairs configurations remain valuable correctness references even though they are unsuitable production defaults for large worlds.

Parallel solving requires an explicit conflict strategy​

Two contacts can update the same body. Letting independent shader invocations write its velocity concurrently creates races. An ordered solver avoids those races but exposes little parallelism, even if it runs entirely on the GPU.

The experimental parallel path computes per-contact changes and then gathers them per body. Each stage has a clear owner for every output. This Jacobi-style approach exposes parallel work but needs relaxation and convergence checks, particularly for deep stacks and large mass ratios. A different option is to color constraints into groups that do not share writable bodies, then process those groups in sequence. Coloring costs work and introduces scheduling choices; it is not automatically faster.

Our acceptance criteria must compare residual error, penetration, energy behavior and runtime together. Making a solver faster by reducing iterations while allowing much larger errors is a different quality setting, not an equivalent performance improvement.

What “fully GPU based” means​

The target is GPU-resident simulation: broad phase, contact generation, solving, integration and pose publication do not require per-step CPU results. It does not require moving every setup task to a shader.

CPU responsibilitiesGPU responsibilities
Parse assets and validate supported semanticsMaintain evolving body state
Cook hulls, mass properties and mesh acceleration dataDiscover candidate pairs and generate contacts
Allocate resources and compile pipelines/graphsSolve contacts and constraints
Submit input commands and schedule fixed stepsIntegrate poses and produce compact events
Consume requested diagnostics or gameplay events asynchronouslyPublish state for GPU rendering

This is the intended boundary. In particular, the glTF example's CPU pose bridge is a remaining integration limitation; a GPU solver alone does not make that whole application GPU-resident.

Readback has two costs: copying data and waiting for the producer. Even a small readback can stall progress if the next simulation step depends on it. Diagnostics should normally be asynchronous and optional. Gameplay events need explicit tick identities and latency semantics; JavaScript cannot synchronously consume a GPU result that has not completed.

Capacity and failure are part of correctness​

GPU queues, pair lists, manifolds and event buffers have finite capacities. Every bounded operation needs an observable overflow or incomplete-work result. A frame that silently drops contacts can look plausible while being physically wrong.

Capacity planning should consider dense piles, extreme size ratios, mesh complexity and contact-cache churn, not just body count. Recovery may require reallocating and rebuilding resources between submissions, retrying from preserved state, or rejecting a scene beyond the declared envelope. A failed step must not be labeled valid merely because it rendered.

The rigid world retains lifetime failure counters on the GPU, so infrequent sampling does not hide an incomplete step. Its simulation still advances when work is incomplete; recovery requires corrected settings and replay from known valid state. Allocation preflight catches device-limit violations before state allocation, but cannot predict all driver memory failures. See capacity and diagnostics.

Synchronization and portability constrain the design​

A workgroup barrier does not synchronize all workgroups. Global dependencies generally require dispatch boundaries and appropriate resource ordering. Fusing stages is useful only when those dependencies remain satisfied. Atomic counters can allocate work, but contention and unpredictable ordering can still limit throughput.

Portable WebGPU code must respect the adapter's storage bindings, buffer sizes and dispatch limits. It cannot assume a CUDA-specific execution model or specialized floating-point atomic operations. Float32 precision also requires declared world scales and tolerances; large coordinates can erase small contact separations. See GPU floating-point precision.

Parallel execution and floating-point rounding complicate deterministic replay. Stable pair ordering and controlled reductions help, but cross-device bitwise reproducibility requires a separate contract and evidence. It is not implied by a fixed timestep.

glTF is an adapter and a qualification workload​

The glTF adapter translates authored shapes, body modes, mass properties, filters and source identities into engine concepts. Simulation kernels should not depend on glTF node indices or special-case a named example. Render-node origins and body centers of mass are different frames and must stay correctly related.

Support has several distinct levels: accepting a schema field, importing it, simulating its intended behavior, and doing so within a qualified performance envelope. Four working example scenes do not prove all four levels for an extension. Draft revisions also need to be pinned; historical sample conventions and newer proposal semantics can differ.

Remaining work includes general moving nonconvex mesh interactions, broad general-body CCD qualification, proposal joints and drives, complete trigger/interactivity semantics, and GPU pose publication through the glTF renderer. Unsupported behavior must remain visible. An asset-specific collision exclusion is not a substitute for implementing the shape pair.

Measuring progress toward state of the art​

GPU acceleration is a workload-dependent result. Small scenes can be dominated by dispatch and synchronization overhead; large independent workloads expose more parallelism. A single connected stack or mechanism can remain difficult despite having many bodies.

Measure latency for one interactive world's step separately from throughput across many worlds. MuJoCo's documentation explicitly distinguishes these goals for MuJoCo Warp. Likewise, PhysX's GPU documentation distinguishes GPU broad phase from GPU dynamics. Accelerating discovery alone does not establish whole-simulation performance.

A useful qualification report records:

  • Accuracy and stability: contact error, penetration, joint drift, conservation where appropriate, energy injection, sleep/wake behavior and timestep convergence.
  • End-to-end cost: CPU encoding, complete GPU step time, render publication latency, queue depth, memory and p50/p95 timings after warm-up.
  • Workload and quality settings: shape distribution, contacts, density, timestep, substeps, iterations, CCD policy, tolerances and any geometry approximation.
  • Device coverage and failure behavior: backend, adapter, limits, overflow counters, unsupported features and explicit fallback policy.

Independent analytical cases and reference implementations provide stronger evidence than visual plausibility alone. Broad-phase-only benchmarks must be labeled accordingly. Competitive claims need equivalent workloads and error budgets, not just matching body counts. This qualification work is what turns a compelling demonstration into a reliable GPU physics library.
