References and credits

luma.gl grows from the work of many people: library authors, graphics researchers, standards contributors, artists, educators, and the developers who maintain the web graphics ecosystem. We are grateful for the code, ideas, tools, and freely available examples that make this project possible.

This guide distinguishes adapted source, research implemented independently, inspiration, standards compatibility, and separately installed dependencies. The package notices contain redistribution terms; the acknowledgments here also recognize contributions that go beyond license requirements.

For feature-specific literature and detailed scientific citations, see Attributions and credits.

Origins and community​

luma.gl began as an ES6 port of Nicolas Garcia Belmonte's PhiloGL, developed at Sencha Labs. Our v1 release history records that origin, and the repository license retains Sencha's notice. PhiloGL's approachable WebGL abstractions and examples provided a foundation for the project. We thank Nicolas and the PhiloGL contributors for making that work available.

Uber Technologies developed and open sourced early luma.gl work; the retained 2015 copyright records that contribution. We thank the luma.gl contributors, including maintainers, reviewers, documentation writers, issue reporters, and example authors, for its continued development. vis.gl and the OpenJS Foundation provide the project's community and organizational home.

Graphics implementations and educational foundations​

  • Evan Wallace made glfx.js available with clear, compact image-processing shaders. The classic adjustment, blur, warp, and artistic filters retain that implementation lineage through luma.gl's shader-pass interfaces. The repository license also acknowledges adapted three.js postprocessing work.
  • Gregg Tavares and the webgpu-utils contributors supplied the mipmap implementation foundation. His web graphics teaching and utilities help make the underlying APIs accessible.
  • Mohamad Moneimne and contributors supplied the historical glTF-WebGL-PBR reference shader. Both GLSL and WGSL adaptations preserve its 2016–2017 MIT terms. The modern Sample Viewer's later Apache license does not replace that historical license.
  • Ricardo Cabello (mrdoob) and the three.js contributors provided the text geometry, font loading, curves, extrusion, and half-float conversion foundations. MAGENTA Ltd. supplied the font data behind Helvetiker; its embedded font permission terms are retained separately from the code's MIT license.
  • Google's TDL contributors supplied the acknowledged plane, sphere, and truncated-cone primitive lineage. Porting, moving, or replacing these helpers does not erase their contribution.
  • NVIDIA's FXAA authors and the Cesium contributors supplied the FXAA 3.11 shader lineage. The retained source records luma.gl's sampling, alpha, and luminance modifications; package notices preserve NVIDIA and Cesium terms.
  • Mikola Lysenko and regl contributors, wwwtyro, and the Babylon.js Spector.js contributors supplied useful WebGL compatibility and debugging foundations. These source adaptations are recorded in the core package notices.
  • Benjamin Wasty supplied gltf-loader-ts schema types, retained through loaders.gl and now adapted in luma.gl’s glTF package. Its MIT notice accompanies both schema files and the package.
  • World Labs and Spark contributors supplied RAD opacity and Gaussian support behavior adapted for luma.gl's paged splat rendering. This acknowledgment describes retained implementation work, beyond compatibility with Spark data.

Research and algorithm references​

Research credit and source-code provenance are complementary. These works explain important ideas used by the rendering and compute features:

  • Brian Karis, Real Shading in Unreal Engine 4; Brent Burley, Physically-Based Shading at Disney; and Christophe Schlick, An Inexpensive BRDF Model for Physically-based Rendering. See the PBR material reference for their relationship to the shader.

  • Eric Heitz, Jonathan Dupuy, Stephen Hill, and David Neubelt, Real-Time Polygonal-Light Shading with Linearly Transformed Cosines, ACM Transactions on Graphics (SIGGRAPH 2016), 35(4), 2016. Their GGX fit data supplies area-light shading tables, pinned to selfshadow/ltc_code commit 31e5e96b54f98f33098f8503003119ba2231a1c6. The full LTC terms and required paper citation ship beside the source and with standalone distribution notices.

  • The PROJ contributors document the projection formulas, unit conversions, and published Krüger coefficient matrices referenced by GPU projection kernels. The projection documentation supplies mathematical references, distinct from source-code adaptation.

  • Bernhard Kerbl, Georgios Kopanas, Thomas Leimkühler, and George Drettakis, 3D Gaussian Splatting for Real-Time Radiance Field Rendering, established the rendering representation behind Gaussian-splat workflows. This citation does not imply redistribution of their reference implementation.

  • NVIDIA and the RAPIDS contributors pioneered GPU-resident analysis through cuDF, cuGraph, cuVS, cuSpatial, cuProj, cuCIM, and cuXfilter. luma.gl's related APIs document independent WebGPU implementations and their differing scope. Crossfilter's authors and contributors also deserve credit for coordinated, interactive filtering; see the crossfilter attribution.

  • Lalit Maganti's description of Perfetto’s batched exponential search inspired the pixel-mipmap foundation described in the GPU trace algorithms.

  • Jeroen van der Zijp's Fast Half Float Conversions is the algorithm reference cited by the three.js-derived half-float conversion utility.

  • Erin Catto and the Box2D contributors provide the simulation timestep reference linked by the GPU physics guide. This is a numerical/educational reference, not a claim that luma.gl redistributes Box2D.

Standards, libraries, and tools​

We thank the W3C GPU for the Web group, The Khronos Group, browser and GPU driver implementers, and contributors to WebGPU, WGSL, WebGL, GLSL, glTF, and ANARI. Their specifications and implementations make portable web graphics possible.

The math.gl, loaders.gl, deck.gl, and probe.gl communities provide mathematics, asset loading, visualization, diagnostics, and shared infrastructure. The Apache Arrow and GeoArrow communities provide interoperable columnar data foundations. Slang, TypeScript, Vitest, Playwright, Vite, Docusaurus, Biome, and Yarn contributors support development and documentation. Separately installed dependencies retain their own licenses; this guide is not a transitive dependency bill of materials.
